Abstract

Laws as runtime constraints on a learned world model

As robotic systems grow more general, legal norms are needed to integrate them into society. This paper extends the isomorphism problem of aligning legal source texts with their encodings, and measures two key challenges to robot normative control: (1) the grounding isomorphism gap, where perception error grounds false atoms for legal reasoning, and (2) the ontological isomorphism gap, where one legal conclusion admits many faithful translations into planning constraints.

The paper introduces a legal planning stack that employs Defeasible Deontic Logic (DDL) to constrain a motion planner. The stack leverages learned world models to plan and to provide legal context, enabling ex ante governance that intervenes before an illegal action is executed. It was deployed on a simulated robot arm pushing a cube across a 3×3 grid. The findings were that (1) the legislated agent abided substantially more often than the non-legislated one, and modeling perception uncertainty lifted abidance even further, (2) the legal reasoning ran efficiently at runtime and its verdicts were auditable, and (3) the stack adapted to exogenous signals and endogenous rule changes. Both gaps were measured: (4) world model and probe error corrupted the factual input for the DDL reasoner, and (5) a single law admitted several faithful metric interpretations yielding drastically different abidance.

The stack

The law enters through perception and leaves through the planner

A DDL rule base encodes the source text, probes ground latent predictions as its facts, and its verdict constrains the RRT planner — closing the loop before an illegal action is executed. The two dashed arrows are where isomorphism breaks down.

Setup

A Franka arm with a welded paddle pushes a 0.09 m cube across a 3×3 grid of 0.133 m cells in Isaac Lab. The planner never sees the cube's pose — only a 224×224 RGB frame and proprioception — so the pose must be read back from pixels by learned probes. Planning is RRT in the latent space of DINO-WM, with horizon H = 3; only the first action of each plan is executed before replanning. Each task starts the cube in one cell with its goal in the diametrically opposite cell, giving eight tasks run over 50 variations each: 400 episodes per agent. The center cell is illegal and is never a start or a goal.

Three agents, one stack

The agent types come from the BIO framework and differ only in what they do with the violation flag on a candidate action — same planner, same world model, same laws. A fourth oracle agent runs the social policy on simulation ground truth instead of probe readings, isolating how much of the failure is perception rather than reasoning.

AgentTreats a deontic conclusion as…
Realistic Nothing. No legislation; returns the lowest-cost plan in the tree.
Social A hard constraint. Violating actions are pruned as the tree is expanded.
Deviant A price. Violations cost λ = 0.067 m of progress each, never a veto.
Oracle A hard constraint, but grounded on ground truth rather than probes.

The laws

A small rule base that still exercises every DDL construct

In natural language: the center cell is off-limits (R2); a yellow sign obliges the cube to first enter a yellow cell (R5); reaching it flips the sign green if the trajectory is clean (R7) or red if the cube already passed through the center (R7b); a green sign then permits the center (R4), while a red sign freezes the agent (R2, R3 ⇒ R9). The yellow cell is a legality check-in — the cube must present itself for authorization before the restricted area opens, like scanning a key card at a checkpoint.

Audit trace

Watching the verdict and the goal switch mid-episode

Every deontic decision is logged with the rule that fired, the atoms grounding it, and the verdict. One panel per decision step of a single social-agent episode, each rendered at the cube's recorded rest position and tinted with the effective sign — the color the verdict was actually assessed under, which at the last step differs from the color on the sign itself.

  2. Step 1yellow The push overshoots by nearly 8 cm and drops the cube in the forbidden center. The plan was legal; the execution was not. This is the grounding gap.
  3. Step 2yellow R10's compensatory duty fires — [O]exit_cell(4). The goal bank retargets to cell 1 and the exit succeeds. The law absorbs the violation rather than rejecting the agent.

  5. Step 4red It reaches the check-in still carrying visited(4). R7b outranks R7, so the sign flips red rather than green, and R9's permission conflict freezes it. The earlier violation is what closes the door.

Results

Ex ante legislation works, and both gaps are measurable

The findings in brief. Full tables, confidence intervals and the runtime audit are in the paper.

The legal layer is what produces compliance

Perception error grounds the law on the wrong cell

Typical error is a sizeable fraction of a cell and the tail exceeds a full cell width, so a cushion narrows the gap but cannot close it. The oracle agent, running the same laws on ground truth, marks the ceiling: the distance between it and the social agent is the price of grounding the law in learned perception, not a limit of the DDL layer.

One law, several faithful readings

Re-scoring the center-cell prohibition alone — scoring the cube's center or its whole footprint, over its full swept path or only at the frames the planner perceives — moves measured abidance by tens of points on the very same episodes. Nothing about the behavior changes; only the interpretation of the rule does. Which reading is correct is a question a legislator drafting the rule would never see, and it lands on the engineer.

Runtime amendment, at negligible cost

Legal design is updateable and need not be fixed a priori. Inserting a new duty mid-episode strands many agents in violation — some holding a license the reverting sign revoked underneath them — and the compensatory duty walks most of them back into a legal cell by the next step without hurting task success.

The stack tracks exogenous change too: the agent enters the center roughly seven times more often when the sign permits it than when it forbids it.

And the reasoning is cheap. A verdict costs 16.6 ms, against the 12.5 s the planner spends on each executed action — about three orders of magnitude less than the search it governs.
